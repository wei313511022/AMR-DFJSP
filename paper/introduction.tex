\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Fleets of autonomous mobile robots (AMRs) increasingly perform internal
transfers in factories and terminals. In the setting studied here, parcels
arriving at inbound doors must be consolidated on capacitated robots and
delivered to outbound stations across a shared floor. The scheduler selects
the carrier and order of each pickup and delivery. Its decisions are executed
in a physical system in which robots require collision-free trajectories and
loading points are exclusive servers with finite waiting space.

Learned constructive dispatchers offer fast inference and generalisation
across scheduling instances. Beginning with L2D~\cite{zhang2020l2d}, this line
encodes the evolving schedule with a graph neural network, commits one action
per decision epoch, and trains from schedule cost. Flexible-job-shop variants
extend the action to operation--machine and operation--machine--vehicle
choices~\cite{song2023fjsp,moon2024hgs,yuan2026gin,matrans2026}. In the cited
transport-aware formulations, vehicle motion is represented primarily by
fixed pairwise travel times. Such a model is efficient, but it omits delays
created by route reservations and by non-preemptive service at shared pickup
and delivery points.

Assignment and routing have also been coupled directly. Integrated MAPD
informs task assignment with routed delivery cost, including for capacitated
robots~\cite{chen2021integrated}; reinforcement-learning frameworks place a
task-assignment policy above a conflict-free path generator or jointly learn
assignment and path-planning agents~\cite{li2024hierarchical,
wang2026collaborative}. These studies establish routing-aware assignment as an
effective basis for robot coordination. Their primary settings are online task
allocation or per-instance optimisation. This paper addresses a complementary
question: how much does an idealised transport model distort the value and
ordering of
\emph{complete offline schedules} when capacity, non-zero service, finite
waiting space, and collision-aware execution act together?

We study this question through a paired comparison. For the same assignment
and operation sequence, an idealised decode charges free-space travel and
starts service on arrival, whereas the deployment executor plans
space--time paths and enforces exclusive service. The resulting
\emph{execution penalty} measures value error without conflating it with a
change in schedule. More importantly, we compare the orderings induced by the
two evaluators: if they disagree, idealised training is not merely
miscalibrated but can prefer the wrong decision. Queueing time and routing
excess are reported as operational diagnostics rather than asserted to form
an additive causal decomposition of makespan.

Executor-relative learning introduces a credit-assignment problem. A complete
schedule has an unambiguous executed makespan, but a partial sequence admits
many completions and therefore has no unique executor-consistent incremental
cost. Dense lower-bound differences used in constructive shop scheduling are
available for the idealised model, not for the terminal objective considered
here. A terminal return remains valid but provides weak action-level credit.
We therefore complete each relevant prefix under a fixed dispatching rule and
evaluate paired policy and baseline completions with the unchanged deployment
executor. A calibrated surrogate is used only to construct state features at
the intermediate decision epochs; it does not replace the executor in the
terminal comparison.

The study uses established graph encoders, composite actions, and
routing-aware learning components to isolate a distinct contribution:
measuring the decision error induced by an idealised transport abstraction
and assigning executor-relative terminal performance to individual
commitments in an offline, service-constrained setting. Specifically, the
paper contributes:

\begin{itemize}
\item A formulation of offline capacitated AMR transfer with class-substitutable
  rack space, exclusive finite-waiting service points, and a fixed
  collision-aware executor, together with a paired execution-penalty metric.
\item An evaluation protocol for both value error and decision error, using
  makespan penalty, schedule-rank correlation, routability, and separately
  recorded queueing and routing delays over controlled fleet-density sweeps.
\item A paired prefix-conditioned rollout estimator in which policy and
  baseline completions are evaluated under the same deployment executor,
  with a calibrated surrogate supplying intermediate state features.
\item A factorial evaluation that separates training signal (idealised or
  executor-relative) from state observability (without or with congestion
  features), together with comparisons at matched computational budget.
\end{itemize}
