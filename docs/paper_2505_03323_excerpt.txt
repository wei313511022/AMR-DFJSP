Unraveling the Rainbow: can value-based methods schedule? Arthur Corrêaa, Alexandre Jesusa, Cristóvão Silvaa and Samuel Moniz a,* aDepartment of Mechanical Engineering, CEMMPRE, ARISE, Universidade de Coimbra, Coimbra, Portugal Abstract. Recently, deep reinforcement learning has emerged as a promising approach for solving complex combinatorial optimization problems. Broadly, deep reinforcement learning methods fall into two categories: policy-based and value-based. While value-based ap- proaches have achieved notable success in domains such as the Ar- cade Learning Environment, the combinatorial optimization commu- nity has predominantly favored policy-based methods, often over- looking the potential of value-based algorithms. In this work, we conduct a comprehensive empirical evaluation of value-based algo- rithms, including the Deep Q-Network and several of its advanced extensions, within the context of two complex combinatorial prob- lems: the job-shop and the flexible job-shop scheduling problems, two fundamental challenges with multiple industrial applications. Our results challenge the assumption that policy-based methods are inherently superior for combinatorial optimization. We show that several value-based approaches can match or even outperform the widely adopted Proximal Policy Optimization algorithm, suggesting that value-based strategies deserve greater attention from the com- binatorial optimization community. Our code is openly available at: https://github.com/AJ-Correa/Unraveling-the-Rainbow. 1 Introduction Historically, combinatorial optimization (CO) problems have been predominantly solved by exact formulations and meta-heuristics [41, 44]. While exact algorithms guarantee optimality with suffi- cient computational time, they are completely intractable for solv- ing medium and large-sized instances [14, 20]. Alternatively, meta- heuristics employ efficient exploration mechanisms to find high- quality solutions within reduced computational time [28, 23]. Still, these methods face significant limitations when applied to large-scale problems due to their non-linear time complexity. This challenge be- comes even more pronounced when dealing with applications where problems must be solved in real-time. In response, deep reinforcement learning (DRL) has emerged in recent years as an alternative solving method to CO problems [3]. By leveraging neural networks, DRL methods learn patterns and trends through interactions with a simulated environment, solving these problems in an end-to-end manner. Generally, DRL algorithms are classified into two main categories: value-based and policy-based methods. Value-based approaches focus on estimating the value of actions in a given state of the environment, where the state reflects the current situation of the problem. The goal is to learn which ac- tions maximize long-term rewards, using the learned value function ∗ Corresponding Author. Email: samuel.moniz@dem.uc.pt to indirectly derive a policy. On the other hand, policy-based methods directly optimize a policy that maps states to actions, without relying on an explicit value estimation. While policy-based methods often excel in environments with high-dimensional action spaces, value- based approaches are often more sample efficient and exhibit lower variance. Among existing DRL algorithms, many advanced value-based methods have demonstrated impressive performance, from which Rainbow has gained particular importance [17]. Rainbow combines six different improvements to the Deep Q-Network (DQN) algo- rithm [25], resulting in a new highly effective agent. Despite these advances, the typical performance benchmarks used in DRL litera- ture are based on the Arcade Learning Environment (ALE) [1]. In contrast, most existing research on CO has focused on policy-based methods [24], such as Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) [31]. Consequently, there is a fundamental knowledge gap regarding the applicability and effectiveness of value-based algorithms in CO do- mains. Although these methods have shown much promise in ALEs, it is still unclear whether they can effectively tackle complex NP-hard problems. Moreover, they often demand significant implementation effort and computational resources, leaving decision-makers uncer- tain about whether the potential benefits justify the added complexity and cost. Motivated by this, we undertake an empirical evaluation study aimed at assessing the performance of several value-based al- gorithms on two complex combinatorial optimization problems: the job-shop scheduling problem (JSSP) and the flexible job-shop scheduling problem (FJSP). In simple terms, these problems involve allocating a series of operations from multiple jobs to specific ma- chines, with the objective of minimizing the makespan — the total time required to complete all jobs. This study is guided by the follow- ing questions: 1) Do advanced value-based algorithms, like Rainbow and its individual components, offer significant improvements over standard DQNs in a complex CO setting? 2) Can value-based algo- rithms compete with policy-based methods in a CO domain? 3) How does the performance of value-based methods vary when applied to tasks with higher flexibility, such as the FJSP , compared to those with a more rigid structure, like the JSSP? 4) How well do differ- ent value-based algorithms generalize across instances with varying sizes and distributions?To address these questions, we propose a het- erogeneous graph neural network (GNN) model that enables the dy- namic activation or deactivation of each individual Rainbow algorith- mic component. We conducted numerous experiments on both ran- domly generated and public benchmark instances, covering a range of different problem sizes. We focus on scheduling problems specifically because, unlike less restrictive CO problems like the vehicle rout- ing and traveling salesman problems, JSSP and FJSP are inherently harder tasks. As a result, they pose the necessary complexity to evaluate how well performance carries over from simpler ALEs to more constrained combinatorial settings. 2 Related work Recently, a variety of DRL-based works have been developed to solve scheduling problems. Zhang et al. [43] proposed Learning to dispatch (L2D), the first size-agnostic JSSP model in the literature. The authors used a graph isomorphism network (GIN) to encode the partial solution states through a disjunctive graph representation, trained using PPO. Han and Yang [15] presented a Dueling Double DQN approach for the JSSP, using a deep convolutional neural net- work to encode the states. However, although this study applied a value-based method, it lacked a direct comparison with policy-based or other value-based approaches, limiting insights into its effective- ness. Later, Park et al. [27] introduced Learning to Schedule, a sim- ilar approach to L2D, also trained with PPO, featuring a customized message passing architecture to account for different edge types. Tas- sel et al. [35] developed a DRL environment for the JSSP using a matrix-based state representation composed of various hand-crafted features. Although their method yielded promising results within a limited training budget, it lacked size scalability, requiring retraining for problems with a different number of jobs. Lei et al. [22] proposed a multi-action Markov decision process (MDP) formulation for the FJSP, aiming at learning two separate policies for operation sequenc- ing and machine assignment decisions. Falkner et al. [11] explored a different direction by learning a policy to guide a search proce- dure for the JSSP. Although trained using a Double DQN algorithm, this work features an improvement-based method, which is far less prevalent in the literature. A more recent contribution by Song et al. [32] represents a significant step forward in FJSP research. They ex- tended the traditional disjunctive graph into a heterogeneous GNN architecture, allowing the model to capture more complex relation- ships between operations and machines. Tassel et al. [36] proposed a novel perspective by leveraging a constraint programming solver to define environment states for the JSSP. Iklassov et al. [18] introduced a recurrent LSTM architecture, trained with a curriculum learning strategy to improve generalization. Yuan et al. [42] presented a new state representation for the FJSP, embedded using a multi-layer per- ceptron. Additionally, in contrast to prior work, they proposed a new action space that simultaneously handles both job routing and ma- chine assignment decisions. Echeverria et al. [10] proposed a differ- ent state representation for the FJSP that incorporates a new job-type node along with traditional operation and machine nodes. Further- more, the authors employ a hand-crafted mechanism to artificially prune the action space, avoiding the selection of actions that would lead to highly suboptimal schedules. More recently, Corrêa et al. [5] compared the performance of Rainbow with the DQN on the FJSP, concluding that there was no significant performance difference be- tween the two. However, their analysis was limited in scope. Notably, the individual contributions of Rainbow’s algorithmic components were not separately evaluated. Moreover, the work did not include any comparison with policy-based approaches. Despite these recent contributions, most research continues to em- phasize policy-based methods, with PPO being particularly promi- nent. While value-based algorithms have demonstrated strong per- formance in ALEs, their efficacy on complex combinatorial prob- lems remains uncertain. To the best of our knowledge, no prior work has systematically evaluated the performance of such algorithms on combinatorial scheduling tasks. 3 Preliminaries 3.1 Problem description Job-shop scheduling problem: A typical JSSP is defined as follows: two sets are given — J = {J1, J2, . . . , Jn} being a set of n jobs and M = {M1, M2, . . . , Mm} a set of m machines. An instance is characterized by the dimensions n × m, indicating the number of jobs and machines, respectively. Each job Ji ∈ J must go through a predefined sequence of operations Oi1 − →Oi2 − →... − →Oim, where each operation Oij can only begin after the completion of the preceding operation Oi(j−1). For each operation Oij, there is a corresponding machine Mk responsible for processing it, with a processing time pij. The problem is subject to further constraints: once a machine starts to process an operation, it must complete it without interruption; each machine can process only one operation at a time, and no operation can be processed by more than one ma- chine simultaneously. Additionally, all jobs and machines are avail- able at the beginning of the scheduling horizon. In this paper, we consider minimizing the makespan Cmax as the objective function, where Cmax = maxi{Ci}, with Ci being the completion time of job Ji ∀ i ∈ {1, 2, ..., n}. In simple terms, the makespan is the total time necessary for the completion of all jobs. Flexible job-shop scheduling problem: The FJSP is an extension of the JSSP that introduces flexibility in machine assignment deci- sions. In a standard JSSP, each operation of a job must be processed on a specific predetermined machine. However, in the FJSP, each operation Oij can be processed by a subset of candidate machines Mij ⊆ M . This added flexibility decouples the strict one-to-one mapping between operations and machines. The FJSP has total flex- ibility if, for every operation across all jobs, the set of candidate ma- chines is equal to M. In contrast, it has partial flexibility if at least one operation is not executable on all possible machines. Similarly to the JSSP, the objective function is to minimize the makespan. All other JSSP constraints also apply to the FJSP. 3.2 Reinforcement Learning In reinforcement learning (RL) problems, one or more agents operate within a defined environment and interact with it over a sequence of discrete timesteps. At each timestep t, the agent observes a state st that reflects the current situation of the environment and selects an action at from the set of possible actions at that point. This action influences the environment, leading to a new state st+1 determined by a transition function P . Finally, the agent is given a reward rt based on the reward functionR(st, at). This loop continues until the problem is solved, marking the end of an epoch. These interactions can be formalized as an MDP [29], which provides a mathematical framework for sequential decision-making

tasks. An MDP is defined by a tuple (S, A, R, P, γ), where: S de- notes the set of states, A represents the set of actions, R is the reward function, P is the transition function and γ is the discount factor. The goal of an RL agent is to maximize its total cumulative re- wards, also known as return. This return can be expressed as Gt =P t γtrt, where γ ϵ [0, 1) is a discount factor that balances the sig- nificance of short-term and long-term rewards. By learning a pol- icy π, the agent aims to maximize its return. Therefore, it is crucial that the reward function aligns with the objective function, ensur- ing that maximizing long-term rewards leads to optimal solutions. In value-based RL, the policy is often derived through a value function Vπ(s) = Eπ[Gt|st = s], which gives the expected discounted return starting from state s and following the policy onward, or an action- value function Qπ(s, a) = Eπ[Gt|st = s, at = a] (also called q-value function). 3.3 Deep Q-Network The DQN algorithm [25] successfully combines q-learning, RL and deep neural networks to handle high-dimensional state-action spaces. Unlike traditional q-learning [40], which uses a table to keep track and update the q-value of each state-action pair — a method that quickly becomes infeasible in large or continuous environments — DQN leverages neural networks to approximate this function. DQNs learn by minimizing a loss function between expected and target q- values, computed as (rt + γmaxat+1 Q(st+1, at+1, θtarget) − Q(st, at, θonline))2 (1) , where θ denotes the parameters (weights) of the neural networks, which are updated via backpropagation. To stabilize learning, DQN uses two separate networks: the online network and the target net- work. The online network is responsible for selecting actions and estimating current q-values, and its parameters are updated at every training step. The target network, on the other hand, is used to com- pute the target q-values in the loss function. Its weights are updated less frequently, by copying the parameters from the online network at regular intervals, which helps to reduce divergence by keeping the targets fixed over short periods. Another key component of the DQN algorithm lies in the use of an experience replay. At each timestep t, the transition tuple (st, at, rt, st+1) is stored inside an experience replay buffer. When computing the loss function, to mitigate bias introduced with the tem- poral correlation between consecutive timesteps, a minibatch of tran- sitions is uniformly sampled from the buffer. Finally, to balance exploration and exploitation, an ϵ-greedy strat- egy is employed. At each timestep, a parameter ϵ ranging from 0 to 1 dictates the probability of selecting actions randomly rather than choosing the action with the highest q-value. Typically,ϵ is annealed over time, prioritizing random actions early on and gradually shifting toward exploitation as learning progresses. 3.4 Rainbow Hessel et al. [17] introduced Rainbow, an agent that combines 6 well- known algorithmic extensions to the original DQN. All extensions, individually and in combination, demonstrated significant improve- ments over the DQN algorithm when applied on the ALE. Next, we provide a brief overview of these extensions and refer readers to the original papers for a more detailed explanation of each. Double Deep Q-Network: In the original DQN, there is an overesti- mation bias since the same function is used for both action selection and evaluation when computing the target q-values. Double DQN [16] mitigates this issue by decoupling action selection from evalua- tion: the online network selects the action, while the target network evaluates its value. Prioritized Experience Replay: Instead of sampling transitions uni- formly, Prioritized Experience Replay (PER) [30] assigns a higher probability to transitions with greater temporal-difference errors, i.e., transitions with greater learning potential. Dueling Networks: Wang et al. [39] proposed Dueling Networks, which modify the original DQN architecture by splitting the net- work into two separate streams: one estimating the value function Vπ(s) and the other computing the advantage function Aπ(s, a) = Qπ(s, a) − Vπ(s), which measures the relative importance of each action. Both streams are then combined to form the network output. Noisy Networks: Unlike the ϵ-greedy exploration strategy used in DQN, Noisy Networks [12] introduce trainable noise into the net- work weights, allowing the model to learn useful perturbations for more efficient exploration. Distributional RL: Instead of learning the expected q-value, Distri- butional RL [2] learns to predict a full distribution of returns, leading to a richer understanding of uncertainty. Multi-step learning: This extension [33] considers rewards over multiple future timesteps rather than just the immediate next reward. Instead of updating the q-value based on a single-step reward, it ac- cumulates rewards over multiple steps before making an update. 4 Unraveling the Rainbow 4.1 Model architecture In our work, we follow the same model architecture designed by Song et al. [32], a heterogeneous GNN. Our choice for using this model as a baseline is two-fold: first, it is a well-consolidated model in the literature, being used as a benchmark across several works [42, 38, 10]; second, its flexibility allows it to be easily adapted to solve JSSP instances without any loss of generality. For a compre- hensive description of the original architecture, we refer the readers to the work of Song et al. [32]. Our goal in this study is to empirically evaluate the performance of different value-based methods. The original heterogeneous GNN architecture was trained using the PPO algorithm, a policy-based ap- proach. To align with our objectives, we made the following modifi- cations to the original work: • In policy-based RL, the model outputs a probability distribution over actions (the policy). In contrast, a value-based approach esti- mates the value for each state-action pair. Thus, to adapt the model to a value-based approach, we removed the softmax function from the final layer of the deep neural network, allowing it to predict q- values instead of probabilities. • We designed an integrated environment that allows for the dy- namic activation or deactivation of each individual extension over the DQN algorithm. By using Boolean flags to control which ex- tensions are applied, we can train the model with any desired com- bination of these enhancements. 4.2 Configuration and baselines Following prior work [17, 26], we evaluate the impact of adding each algorithmic extension to the DQN. Models were trained for 3000 epochs on the smaller in- stances (6×6, 10×5, 20×5), and for 5000 epochs on the larger ones (15×10 and 20×10). To mitigate overfitting, a new training instance was randomly generated at the start of each epoch. Details of the instance generation process are provided in the following section. Throughout training, we performed a validation step each time the target network was updated. To conduct all testing experiments, we selected the model that achieved the lowest average makespan across all validation steps dur- ing training. We then evaluated each different model on 100 distinct testing instances. In addition to the randomly generated instances, we also evaluated the models on standard public benchmark datasets. For the JSSP, we used the Taillard [34], Demirkol [9] and Lawrence [21] datasets; for the FJSP, we adopted the Brandimarte [4] and Dauzère–Paulli [8] benchmarks. In all experiments, inference was performed using a greedy decoding. To benchmark the results of all algorithmic variants, we used Google OR-Tools [13], a powerful constraint programming algo- rithm widely used in the literature. We set a time limit of 45 minutes for solving each individual testing instance. For public benchmark problems, we used the best known solutions in the literature [43, 42]. We also include results on the original heterogeneous GNN model [32], trained with the PPO algorithm. To ensure a fair comparison, we trained this model for the same number of epochs (or iterations, in this case) as all value-based algorithms, and kept the hyperparam- eters unchanged from the original work. All models were trained on an Ubuntu v24.04 machine with 32GB of RAM, an Intel Core i9-13900 and an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4060. 4.3 Instance generation To generate all training, validation and testing instances, we followed different procedures for the JSSP and FJSP due to the distinct nature of each problem. For the JSSP, we used the same method as Zhang et al. [43]. The procedure is straightforward since the JSSP does not in- volve machine flexibility. Each job consists of exactlym operations, where m is the number of machines in the problem. For every job, operations are assigned to a randomly selected permutation of the available machines, ensuring that no machine is repeated within the same job. The processing times for these operations are then drawn uniformly from the range [1, 99]. For the FJSP, we followed the approach used by Song et al. [32]. Each job Ji has a number of operations ni. The value of ni is uni- formly sampled on different ranges based on the number of machines in the problem: [4, 6] for instances with 5 machines, [5, 7] for 6 ma- chines and [8, 12] for 10 machines. For each operation Oij, a set of candidate machines Mij is sampled, with the number of candidates ranging from 1 to the total number of machines in the problem. An average processing time ¯pij is also drawn uniformly from the interval [1, 20]. The actual processing time for operation Oij on each candi- date machine Mk is then sampled from a uniform distribution over the range [0.8¯pij, 1.2¯pij]. 4.4 Hyperparameters The algorithmic extensions introduced significantly expand the hy- perparameter search space compared to the original DQN algorithm. Consequently, performing an exhaustive hyperparameter search be- comes impractical. In our study, we initially adopted the default val- ues reported in the original papers for each extension. We then man- ually adjusted them based on their sensitivity, focusing on larger in- stances, which exhibited greater training instability. We set the exploration rate to start at 1, with an exponential decay of e−eps/600, where eps denotes the epoch number. The exploration rate decreases until reaching a minimum value of 0.1, from which point it is maintained until the end of training. Across all scenarios, we used a learning rate of 2 × 10−4 with the Adam optimizer [19]. The minibatch size was set to 32, proving to be a lot more stable than using larger batches (which failed to converge on larger problems). The target network was updated every 10 epochs. We set the discount factor to 0.99, and the experience replay buffer capacity to 20000 transitions. For the PER, we set the prioritization exponent to 0.4. The importance sampling exponent began at 0.4 and increased lin- early throughout training, reaching 1 by the end. In Multi-step learn- ing, the update target was set to 4 for the FJSP and 2 for the JSSP, as higher values led to convergence issues in this problem. For the Distributional RL extension, we used 51 atoms. The minimum and maximum values used for probability mass calculations were highly sensitive hyperparameters. Incorrect tuning led to significant insta- bility during training, so we manually adjusted these values based on the problem type (JSSP or FJSP) and instance size. 4.5 Results on randomly generated instances In this section, we analyze our results through the lens of questions 1, 2, and 3 outlined at the end of Section 1:Do advanced value-based algorithms, like Rainbow and its individual components, offer signif- icant improvements over standard DQNs in a complex CO setting? On average, 6 out of the 8 value-based methods performed better than the PPO algorithm. The third question addresses task flexibility and its influence on algorithm performance. Results suggest a clear distinction between both problem variants. For the JSSP, a relatively rigid problem with fixed operation routes and limited action spaces, value-based meth- ods underperformed compared to PPO, especially as problem size in- creased. In contrast, results on the FJSP were much more promising. In this setting, most value-based approaches outperformed the PPO model. This difference may be attributed to the inherently greater complexity and flexibility of the FJSP, which can be decomposed into two hierarchical decision levels (operation sequencing and machine assignment). Policy-based RL is known to exhibit a higher variance than value-based algorithms. Therefore, it is possible that the PPO algorithm is better suited in a more rigid and deterministic setting, such as the JSSP. Given the fixed routing and limited action choices on JSSP, the higher variance typically associated with policy-based methods may be less detrimental, allowing PPO to learn more ef- fective policies. These findings indicate that value-based and policy- based methods can have different strengths depending on the nature of the problem — specifically, its structure, flexibility, and action space complexity. 4.6 Results on benchmark instances In this section, we address the fourth question presented in Section 1: How well do different value-based algorithms generalize across in- stances with varying sizes and distributions? To investigate this, we evaluate the cross-size and cross-distribution generalization of all previously discussed models on a collection of well-known pub- lic benchmark datasets. These datasets include instances of varying sizes and are derived from distributions distinct from those seen dur- ing training. This setup provides a strong foundation for assessing how well each model generalizes beyond their original training set- ting. For clarity and better visualization of results, we focus on mod- els trained on 6×6, 10×5, and 20×10 instances, as these configura- tions demonstrated a better average performance compared to those trained on 20×5 and 15×10 instances. All results are shown in Ta- ble 3. On the JSSP benchmarks, the DDQN and PER variants consis- tently outperformed the DQN, indicating that these extensions offer better generalization across varying problem sizes and distributions. While other extensions generally outperformed DDQN and PER on same-size, same-distribution instances (i.e., instances similar to the training data), they tended to perform worse on benchmark datasets. Nevertheless, many of these extensions still outperformed the base- line DQN in multiple cases, though not as consistently as DDQN and PER. On the FJSP, the improvements achieved by Rainbow exten- sions were less pronounced compared to their results on randomly generated instances. Although various extensions showed superior performance than DQN on Dauzère’s instances, the DQN generally outperformed all models on Brandimarte’s dataset. This indicates that certain architectural variants may overfit to specific features of the training data, limiting their generalization to structurally different problems.

Average training runtime (minutes) for each RL algorithm. 5 Conclusion and future work In this work, we have demonstrated that value-based RL holds poten- tial for solving complex CO problems. Our experiments revealed that several value-based algorithms not only competed with, but in many cases outperformed the PPO algorithm. These findings challenge the prevailing assumption that policy-based methods are inherently supe- rior for CO, suggesting that value-based methods deserve more atten- tion from the community. Among the value-based methods studied, we evaluated both the classic DQN and a set of advanced algorithmic extensions. While various extensions often improved upon the base- line DQN, their gains were generally modest, and not always consis- tent across different problem types. Replicating the effectiveness of these methods in complex combinatorial environments proved to be challenging, highlighting the need for further research into their ap- plication beyond the ALE. Given the substantial implementation ef- fort these extensions require, understanding their true practical value in real-world CO problems is crucial for decision-makers. Looking ahead, we plan to expand our analysis to more sophisti- cated value-based methods, such as Munchausen RL [37], quantile regression DQN [7] and implicit quantile networks [6]. We are also interested in investigating the impact of problem complexity on al- gorithmic performance, extending our evaluation to richer and more realistic scheduling problems like the job-shop with setup times, ma- chine breakdowns, and due dates.
