v10 factorial · 2 × 3 · seed 44 · 100 instances per size

The cheap reward wins

Six training configurations crossing what the policy can see against what it is scored on. The configuration that trains against a 290×-cheaper reward beats the one that trains against the real executor — at every workload above n = 20, on every seed.

The design

Two factors, six cells

The mask axis removes every congestion channel the encoder can see: how long each door stays busy, how many robots are queued, door saturation, remaining service, committed workload, the wait this action expects, and how many robots are nearby. Masked, the policy still sees position, distance, inventory and time — but cannot tell that any door is busy.

The return axis changes what the schedule is scored on during training. C̃ charges free-space travel with no queueing and cannot fail to route. Ψ̂ is the calibrated fast model the rollout already advances — 7.0% value error and τ = 0.736 against the executor at n = 60. Φ is the real space-time A* executor, and costs 290× Ψ̂ per schedule.

F recovers the number you remembered — honestly

The −8.34% F posts at n = 60 essentially matches the −8.56% you remembered from v8 — but that figure came from a single cherry-picked seed at K = 16, while this is three seeds averaged at K = 8. Same result, half the search budget, no seed selection.

Losing one factor is survivable; losing both is not

Masking costs 35.5 units at n = 100 when the reward is the executor, 74.8 when it is the surrogate, and 434.6 when it is the idealised decode. The penalty for blindness scales with how uninformative the reward already is.

Read the other way: with full features, the cheap reward is better (F − D = −15.6). With features masked, the expensive reward is needed (E − C = +23.7). The two factors substitute — a policy needs congestion information from somewhere, either its inputs or its reward, and A has neither.

At n = 20 masking is mildly helpful in every row. With 1.25 parcels per robot there is almost no contention to observe, so the congestion channels are noise that costs capacity.

Why

Why the cheaper reward trains a better policy

The most plausible explanation is self-consistency. The policy's features are computed by Ψ̂. Scoring it with Ψ̂ makes the whole decision process coherent: the yardstick it is judged by is exactly the world it can see.

Swapping in Φ makes the reward reflect things the policy has no way to observe — which robot blocked which in an aisle. That information never enters the state, so it can only arrive as gradient noise. It matches what the fidelity study already measured: Ψ̂ ranks candidate schedules almost as well as the executor (τ = 0.736, regret 1.24%) with a value bias that is small and consistent in direction, so as a reward it carries nearly the same ordering at a fraction of the variance.

The practical consequence is large: the reward is 290× cheaper per schedule, and the resulting policy is better.

Caveats

What this does and does not establish

  • Only seed 44 has all six cells. A and B's seeds 42/43 were within ~50 epochs of finishing and C's were at 45% when this ran, so the six-cell table is single-seed. D, E and F have three finished seeds and carry the seed-averaged claims.
  • Selection is held fixed. Every cell uses its _best checkpoint, selected on the executor over the same val_mix set, so cells differ in training and not in how their checkpoint was picked. The _bestideal / _bestsurr checkpoints — each arm selected inside its own paradigm — are not in this table.
  • Masks match training. A, C and E are evaluated with L3 installed, because a checkpoint fed channels it never saw during training is not the model that was trained.
  • Instances. test_case/v3/trend/full_{n}.jsonl, 100 per size, m = 16, verified disjoint from every training and selection pool.
  • Not tested: whether F's advantage survives at n > 100, and whether best-of-16 widens it. Both are cheap to check.
